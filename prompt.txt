SECTION 1 — THE PROBLEM
What is the Enron Problem?
Enron was an American energy company that collapsed in 2001 in one of the largest corporate fraud scandals in history. Executives were hiding billions of dollars in debt using fake accounting tricks. The FBI seized over 600,000 internal emails when investigating.
Here's the key insight: the truth was always in the emails. Executives were writing to each other about deals, write-downs, SEC filings, attorney consultations — all the things that later became evidence. If someone had a tool to automatically find these "disclosure" emails in real time, the fraud could have been caught earlier.
That's exactly what your project builds.
Why does this matter beyond Enron?
Every major organization — banks, hospitals, government agencies — faces the same problem: employees communicate over email, and within millions of routine messages, there are a small number of critical ones that signal fraud, legal risk, financial exposure, or insider threats. Manual review is impossible at scale. Your system automates that detection.

SECTION 2 — BACKGROUND CONCEPTS
Let me teach you every concept you need, from zero.
The Enron Dataset
The dataset contains ~500,000 real emails from ~150 Enron employees, mostly senior management. Researchers use it because it's one of the only real corporate email datasets that's publicly available. Most corporate emails are private. Enron's were released through legal proceedings.
Your project uses a preprocessed, silver-labeled subset of ~10,000 emails stored as a Parquet file. "Silver-labeled" means the labels were generated programmatically (e.g., by rules or a weaker model), not by a human expert reading every email. This introduces some label noise.
What is a "Disclosure"?
In your project, a disclosure is an email that reveals sensitive, non-public, or legally significant information. You've defined five categories:

NONE — routine ("let's meet at 3pm")
STRATEGIC — mergers, acquisitions, restructuring decisions
RELATIONAL — interpersonal coordination between employees
LEGAL — SEC filings, attorney communication, regulatory matters
FINANCIAL — earnings figures, write-downs, balance sheet items

What is NLP?
Natural Language Processing is teaching computers to understand human language. An email is just a string of characters to a computer. NLP turns that string into something a model can reason about — whether by counting words, understanding grammar, or capturing meaning.
What is Classification?
Classification means putting something into a category. In your case: given an email, output either "Disclosure / Not Disclosure" (binary) or "NONE / STRATEGIC / RELATIONAL / LEGAL / FINANCIAL" (multiclass).
What is TF-IDF?
Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency. A way to convert text into numbers.

TF: How often does word "merger" appear in this email?
IDF: How rare is "merger" across all emails?

Words that appear often in one email but rarely elsewhere get high scores. "The" appears everywhere — low score. "Write-down" appears in few emails — high score. This is how TF-IDF captures meaning without understanding language.
What are Embeddings?
An embedding is a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a word or sentence. The key property: words with similar meanings get similar numbers. "Merger" and "acquisition" would be close together in embedding space. "Merger" and "football" would be far apart.
What is BERT?
BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is a model pre-trained on billions of web pages. It understands context. The word "lead" means different things in "lead a team" vs "lead pipe." BERT figures this out by reading both directions simultaneously.
In your project, BERT reads an email and produces a 768-dimensional vector that captures the email's meaning. You then attach a small classification layer on top to make predictions.
What is BiLSTM?
A Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory network. An older architecture (pre-BERT) that reads a sequence of words forward and backward, maintaining a "memory" of what it's seen. It's weaker than BERT because it wasn't pre-trained on massive data, but it's faster to train.
What is XGBoost?
XGBoost is a gradient boosting algorithm — essentially an ensemble of decision trees where each tree corrects the mistakes of the previous one. It works extremely well on tabular/sparse data like TF-IDF matrices. In your results, XGBoost achieved the highest recall (0.9801) among ML models.
What is Threshold Analysis?
Every probabilistic classifier outputs a probability: "I'm 67% sure this is a disclosure." The threshold is the cutoff — above it, you predict "Disclosure"; below it, "Not Disclosure."
The default threshold is 0.5. But for fraud detection, missing a real disclosure (False Negative) is far worse than flagging a normal email (False Positive). So you move the threshold lower — say 0.35 — which catches more real disclosures at the cost of more false alarms. Your phase5_evaluate.py implements four threshold strategies: Youden, F1-optimal, G-mean, and cost-sensitive.

pythondef clean_text(text):

Why remove numbers? Because "we lost $47,382,901" and "we lost $99,231,445" carry the same meaning — financial loss. Replacing both with NUM lets the model learn the pattern without memorizing specific figures.

vectorizers/ml_vectorizer.py
pythondef vectorize(X_train_txt, X_test_txt, X_train_hf, X_test_hf):
    tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(
        max_features=7000,
        ngram_range=(1, 2),    # unigrams AND bigrams
        min_df=3,              # word must appear in 3+ emails
        max_df=0.95,           # ignore words in 95%+ of emails
        sublinear_tf=True      # use log(tf) instead of tf
    )
    X_tr_tfidf = tfidf.fit_transform(X_train_txt)
    X_te_tfidf = tfidf.transform(X_test_txt)

    scaler = StandardScaler()
    X_train_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X_train_hf)

    X_tr = hstack([X_tr_tfidf, csr_matrix(X_train_scaled)])
    X_te = hstack([X_te_tfidf, csr_matrix(X_test_scaled)])
    return X_tr, X_te, tfidf
Why bigrams (ngram_range=(1,2))? "Special purpose entity" is a key Enron phrase. If you only look at unigrams, you see "special", "purpose", "entity" — each common individually. The bigram "special purpose" is rare and highly discriminative.
Why sublinear_tf=True? If "earnings" appears 10 times in an email, does it mean 10x more "financial" than an email where it appears once? No. Log scaling dampens this: log(10) ≈ 2.3. More realistic.
Why StandardScaler on hand features? The hand features have different scales. f_word_count might be 500, f_disclosure_ratio might be 0.003. Without scaling, the word count dominates. StandardScaler makes every feature have mean=0 and std=1.
The hstack: This horizontally stacks the 7,000-column TF-IDF matrix with the 12-column hand features into one 7,012-column matrix. This is the actual input to your ML models.

models/ml_models.py
pythondef get_model(name, class_ratio=None):
    spw = (1.0 / class_ratio) if class_ratio else 0.35

    if name == 'lr':
        return LogisticRegression(
            class_weight='balanced',  # penalizes majority class
            C=1.0,                    # regularization
            solver='lbfgs'
        )
    elif name == 'rf':
        return RandomForestClassifier(
            n_estimators=300,
            class_weight='balanced_subsample'  # per-tree reweighting
        )
    elif name == 'xgb':
        return XGBClassifier(
            scale_pos_weight=spw,  # for binary: neg/pos ratio
            objective='binary:logistic'
        )
Why class_weight='balanced'? Your dataset has more disclosures than non-disclosures (2.83 ratio). Without balancing, the model learns "just say Disclosure for everything — I'll be right 74% of the time." Balanced weighting makes errors on minority class examples count more during training.
Why scale_pos_weight for XGBoost? XGBoost handles class imbalance differently — it needs the ratio explicitly. For binary: scale_pos_weight = num_negatives / num_positives.

models/dl_models.py — BiLSTM
pythonclass BiLSTMBinary(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab_size=10000, embed_dim=128, hidden_dim=128):
        self.embedding = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, embed_dim, padding_idx=0)
        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(embed_dim, hidden_dim, num_layers=2,
                            bidirectional=True, dropout=0.4)
        self.layer_norm = nn.LayerNorm(hidden_dim * 2)
        self.dropout = nn.Dropout(0.4)
        self.fc = nn.Linear(hidden_dim * 2, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        x = self.embedding(x)
        _, (h, _) = self.lstm(x)
        h = torch.cat([h[-2], h[-1]], dim=1)  # concat fwd + bwd
        h = self.layer_norm(h)
        h = self.dropout(h)
        return self.fc(h)
The forward pass, step by step:

embedding(x) — convert token IDs to 128-dim vectors
lstm(x) — process sequence bidirectionally, return hidden states
torch.cat([h[-2], h[-1]]) — grab last layer's forward and backward hidden states, concatenate into 256-dim vector
Layer norm + dropout — regularization
fc(h) — linear layer to get single logit

Why BiLSTM without pre-trained embeddings performs worst? The embeddings are randomly initialized. BERT's embeddings are trained on billions of sentences. Your BiLSTM starts knowing nothing about language; BERT starts knowing English. With only 8,000 training emails, there isn't enough data to learn good embeddings from scratch.

phase5_evaluate.py — The evaluation engine. This is one of your most sophisticated files.
pythondef find_optimal_threshold(y_test, y_prob, method='youden'):
    if method == 'youden':
        j = tpr - fpr
        idx = np.argmax(j)       # maximize true positives, minimize false positives equally
    elif method == 'f1':
        f1s = 2*p*r / (p+r)
        idx = np.argmax(f1s)     # maximize F1
    elif method == 'cost':
        cost_fn, cost_fp = 3, 1
        costs = cost_fn * (1-tpr) + cost_fp * fpr
        idx = np.argmin(costs)   # false negatives cost 3x more
Cost-sensitive threshold: This encodes domain knowledge directly into the decision rule. In corporate disclosure detection, missing a real disclosure (False Negative) is 3x worse than a false alarm. The threshold moves lower to catch more true disclosures.

multiclass_pipeline/utils/focal_loss.py
pythonclass MulticlassFocalLoss(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, logits, targets):
        log_pt = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        log_pt = log_pt.gather(1, targets.unsqueeze(1)).squeeze(1)
        pt = log_pt.exp()
        focal_weight = (1 - pt) ** self.gamma   # gamma=2
        loss = -focal_weight * log_pt
        return loss.mean()
Focal Loss was invented for object detection (finding small objects in images) but works perfectly here. The key idea: (1 - pt)^gamma down-weights easy examples. If the model is already 95% confident on "NONE" emails, their loss contributes almost nothing. Hard examples — ambiguous LEGAL vs FINANCIAL emails — dominate training. This is why focal loss helps with class imbalance.

Why This Specific Architecture?
Why two pipelines (binary + multiclass) instead of one?
Because they solve different problems. Binary is a high-recall filter: "Is there anything sensitive in this email?" Multiclass is a semantic sorter: "If yes, what kind of sensitive?" In production, you'd run binary first, then multiclass on flagged emails only.
Why use hand-crafted features alongside TF-IDF?
TF-IDF is purely frequency-based. It sees "attorney" as just a word. The hand-crafted features encode meaning: "this email mentions attorney-level terms AND uses modal verbs AND has high uncertainty language" — that's a legal disclosure pattern TF-IDF can't capture alone.
Why four threshold strategies?
Different deployment contexts need different tradeoffs:

Regulatory audit → cost-sensitive (recall is everything)
Balanced review → F1-optimal
Equal error → Youden
Empirical → G-mean

Showing all four demonstrates methodological rigor.

SECTION 5 — THEORY-TO-CODE LINKING
BERT: Theory → Code
Theory: BERT uses a Transformer encoder with 12 layers. Each layer has multi-head self-attention that lets every word attend to every other word simultaneously. Pre-training on Masked Language Modeling teaches it language understanding. Fine-tuning on your data teaches it disclosure classification.
In your code:

The attention_mask tells BERT which tokens are real vs padding. Without it, BERT would waste attention on padding zeros.
BERT's config.json shows: hidden_size: 768, num_hidden_layers: 12, num_attention_heads: 12. Each of 12 heads learns different aspects of attention — one might focus on syntactic relationships, another on semantic ones.
XGBoost: Theory → Code
Theory: An ensemble of decision trees where each tree corrects the residuals of all previous trees. Extremely effective on sparse matrices like TF-IDF.
In your code:
pythonXGBClassifier(
    scale_pos_weight=spw,      # handles imbalance
    n_estimators=300,          # 300 trees
    max_depth=6,               # each tree can split 6 levels deep
    learning_rate=0.05,        # small steps prevent overfit
    subsample=0.8,             # each tree sees only 80% of rows
    colsample_bytree=0.8       # each tree sees only 80% of features
)
subsample and colsample_bytree are regularization — they introduce randomness so trees don't all make the same mistakes.

SECTION 8 — VIVA PREPARATION
Q: Why did you use silver labels instead of manually annotating the data?
A: Manual annotation of 10,000 emails would require domain experts familiar with corporate law and finance, and would take weeks. Silver labeling uses heuristics and weaker models to label at scale. The tradeoff is label noise — approximately 5-15% of labels may be incorrect. This is acknowledged in the conclusion and is a known limitation. Future work would include a human-verified gold standard on a subset.
Q: Your BiLSTM has random embeddings. Why not use GloVe?
A: This is an identified gap. The current implementation has the embedding layer in place but initializes randomly. GloVe or FastText embeddings pre-trained on financial text (e.g., Bloomberg financial news corpus) would give the model a semantic head start, likely closing 50-70% of the performance gap with BERT. This is mentioned as future work.
Q: Why is XGBoost's recall 0.98 but BERT's is only 0.88?
A: XGBoost is evaluated at a cost-sensitive threshold (lower than 0.5), which trades precision for recall. BERT is evaluated at its F1-optimal threshold, which balances the two. If BERT is evaluated at the same cost-sensitive threshold, its recall would also approach 0.97+. This is a threshold comparison issue, not a model capability difference.
Q: Your dataset has temporal imbalance. How does this affect results?
A: October 2001 alone has 1,087 emails — 10.9% of the dataset. The model has seen crisis-period language in disproportionate detail. When it encounters a pre-crisis business email, it may over-predict disclosure because the language resembles crisis-period communication patterns. Temporal cross-validation — training on pre-crisis emails and testing on crisis-period emails — would give a more honest assessment of generalization. This is a key future work direction.
Q: What is focal loss and why did you use it?
A: Focal Loss modifies standard cross-entropy by adding a factor (1 - pt)^gamma. When the model is already confident about a prediction (high pt), this factor approaches zero, so the loss contribution is minimal. When the model is uncertain (low pt), the factor approaches 1, making those examples dominate training. With gamma=2, easy examples are effectively down-weighted by a factor of up to 100x. This is critical for imbalanced multiclass classification where NONE emails (majority) would otherwise dominate gradient updates and the model would learn to ignore STRATEGIC and LEGAL categories.
Q: How does the threshold analysis work technically?
A: For a binary classifier, predict_proba() returns a probability for each email. We evaluate all possible threshold values from 0 to 1. For each threshold, we compute precision, recall, and F1. The Youden method picks the threshold maximizing TPR - FPR. The F1 method maximizes the harmonic mean of precision and recall. The cost-sensitive method minimizes 3×FN_rate + 1×FP_rate, encoding domain knowledge that missed disclosures are 3x worse than false alarms.
Q: Why use hstack with sparse matrices?
A: TF-IDF vectors are sparse — 7,000 columns but most are zero (an email uses maybe 200 unique words). Storing them as dense arrays would require gigabytes of RAM. scipy.sparse.hstack keeps them sparse while appending the 12 dense hand features (converted to sparse via csr_matrix). This allows training on a laptop.
Q: How would you deploy this system?
A: The trained models (joblib files for ML, .pt/.safetensors for DL) would be loaded into a REST API (Flask/FastAPI). Incoming emails are preprocessed with the same pipeline, vectorized with the saved TF-IDF vocabulary, and scored. For BERT inference, batch processing is more efficient than single-email queries. A confidence threshold determines whether the email enters a human review queue. Monitoring for distribution shift (if email language patterns change) would be needed for production robustness.
Q: What is the significance of using both precision-recall curves AND ROC curves?
A: ROC curves plot TPR vs FPR. When classes are imbalanced, FPR can look artificially low because the denominator (total negatives) is large. A model that labels everything as Disclosure might still have a decent-looking ROC curve. PR curves are immune to this — they only look at the positive class. Your dataset's 2.83 imbalance ratio is moderate, so both metrics are informative. For more extreme imbalance (say, 1:100), PR-AUC would be the only trustworthy metric.
